Why you feel so bad

You live in a world that isn't built for humans. Feeling bad is the normal reaction.

Evolutionary mismatch is what happens when an animal shaped for one world has to live in another.

It is hard to see in yourself, but easy to see in other animals.

The colour is our reading of how the animal feels: red while it gets no answer, teal once it does. The studies measured what the animals do. That a polar bear needs to move on is the researchers' explanation for the laps, and nobody has measured it in a bear. Some strains of hen sit where others pace.

Nobody thinks the animals are the problem.

Humans are animals too. The same thing is happening to you.

A feeling is there to make you act. You act, the world answers, and the feeling stops. That's a closed loop.

In the tank and in the flat nothing answers, so the feeling keeps going. That's an open loop.

Sometimes an answer does come, and it is a fake.

Sea turtles hatch on a beach at night, and the hatchlings have to find the sea.

The hatchling only checks where the open, bright horizon is.

The same thing happens to you in an office. You know your own people by two things: the same faces every day and work you do together. An office has both.

That a human treats an office as its own people is our reading. No study in Cor tests it.

The page has more cases here. They are left out of this sample.

A bad feeling that gets no answer stays on, and the days pile up.

Then it gets a name: anxiety, burnout, depression. The name says something is wrong with you.

Doctors, coaches and apps each sell you a different fault to fix in yourself.
